# Listening as a craft

A spoken essay is not a blog post with a microphone pointed at it. The ear has no back button that feels free. If a sentence asks the listener to hold too many clauses at once, the meaning falls on the floor and nobody stops to pick it up.

Write for breath. Aim for sentences that a person can say in one lungful. Thirty words is a practical ceiling, not a law of nature. Past that, the host starts stacking asides, and the listener starts waiting for a period that does not come.

Cadence does more work than vocabulary. Short sentence. Then a longer one that earns its length. Then a turn. Joke or warning, keep the turn audible. Lists are fine if you announce that a list is coming. Nested parentheses are not fine. The listener cannot hear a parenthesis.

Numbers need a home. “Up a lot” is not a number. “Forty percent” is a number, and it must appear in a source you actually read. If you cannot point at the line, do not say the figure. The same rule applies to quotations. A phrase in quote marks is a promise that someone said those words. If the promise is fake, the show is fake.

Family stories are a special trap for generated audio. A model will happily invent a mother, a child, a hometown. Listeners treat those details as real because the voice sounds intimate. If the corpus does not contain the family, the script must not either.

The craft is subtraction. Cut the throat-clearing. Cut “let’s dive in.” Cut the summary of the summary. Leave one image, one claim, and one next step. Then stop talking while the listener still wants one more sentence. That is how a daily show stays daily.
